# Write Job Statement Workflow

I'll guide you through crafting a complete, validated job statement that captures what your customers are truly hiring your product to do. A good job statement is not a feature description, a use case, or a user story. It is a precise articulation of the progress a customer is trying to make in a specific situation — including the functional, emotional, and social dimensions of that progress.

## When to Use

- After conducting at least 3 switch interviews and identifying patterns in the struggling moment
- When you need to validate that you understand the job before designing solutions
- When you want to check whether different customer segments share the same job or have distinct jobs
- When positioning or messaging feels off and you suspect it's because you haven't named the job precisely

## Step 1: Gather Raw Material

Use `AskUserQuestion` to understand what you're working with:

**Question:** "What customer data do you have? Walk me through what you know about the struggling moment and what these customers were trying to accomplish."

**Follow-ups:**
- How many interviews have you done?
- Can you describe the recurring struggling moment in 1-2 sentences?
- What did customers say they were hoping would change?
- What language did customers use to describe the problem?

If you have interview data, pull out:
- The struggling moment quotes from each interview
- What customers said they wanted to accomplish (explicit statements)
- What customers said they were frustrated by (Push forces)
- What customers imagined the better state would look like (Pull forces)
- The language customers used naturally — not your paraphrase, their words

Lay it all out before drafting anything. The job statement should emerge from the data, not be imposed on it.

---

## Step 2: Identify the Situation Component

The job statement begins with the situation: "When [situation]..."

The situation is not demographic information. It is a specific set of circumstances that creates the job. Two customers with identical demographics can have completely different jobs depending on their situation.

**Questions to identify the situation:**
- Where and when does this job arise? What context triggers it?
- What just happened that makes this job urgent right now?
- Is this a recurring situation or a one-time event?
- What other things are true about the context when this job arises?

**Situation examples — good vs. weak:**

| Weak Situation | Strong Situation |
|----------------|-----------------|
| "When I need to manage my time" | "When my calendar is overbooked and I'm about to miss a commitment" |
| "When I'm planning a trip" | "When I need to plan a trip for a group with conflicting preferences and I'm the one responsible" |
| "When I need to communicate at work" | "When I need to share bad news with my manager before they hear it from someone else" |
| "When our team collaborates" | "When a new team member needs to get up to speed on a project that's already in progress" |

The situation should be specific enough that a customer reading it says "yes, that's exactly when this happens for me" — not "I guess that could be me sometimes."

**Draft the situation component:**

```
SITUATION DRAFTS

Version 1: "When _______________"
Version 2: "When _______________"
Version 3: "When _______________"

Test each version: Does this describe a specific, recurring circumstance?
Does this distinguish the job from similar-but-different jobs?
Selected: _______________
```

## Step 3: Identify the Motivation Component

The motivation is the core of the job: "I want to [motivation]..."

The motivation is the progress the customer is trying to make — not the feature they want, not the task they're performing, but the underlying move they're trying to accomplish in their life or work. This is the hardest component to get right because customers (and product teams) tend to express it at the task level rather than the progress level.

**Questions to identify the motivation:**
- What were customers trying to accomplish that wasn't working?
- If we went one level of abstraction above the task, what is the real move?
- What would customers say if you asked "but why do you want that?"
- What is the change in state they're trying to create?

**Abstraction check — the "but why?" ladder:**

Task level: "I want to save this document"
One level up: "I want to make sure I don't lose my work"
One more level: "I want to feel secure that my progress is preserved"
One more level: "I want to be able to pick up where I left off without anxiety"

The right level for the motivation is usually 1-2 steps above the task level. Too task-level and you're describing a feature. Too abstract and you lose specificity.

**Motivation examples — good vs. weak:**

| Weak Motivation | Strong Motivation |
|----------------|-------------------|
| "I want to use a better tool" | "I want to move faster without sacrificing accuracy" |
| "I want to track my expenses" | "I want to feel like I'm in control of what I'm spending" |
| "I want to communicate better" | "I want to make sure my message lands the way I intend it" |
| "I want to collaborate" | "I want everyone working on this to have the same picture of where things stand" |

**Draft the motivation component:**

```
MOTIVATION DRAFTS

From interview data — what customers said:
  - "_______________"
  - "_______________"
  - "_______________"

Abstraction analysis:
  Task level: _______________
  One level up: _______________
  Right level: _______________

Version 1: "I want to _______________"
Version 2: "I want to _______________"
Version 3: "I want to _______________"

Test: Is this a desire for progress, not a feature request?
Does it feel like something a customer would say, not a product manager would write?
Selected: _______________
```

## Step 4: Identify the Expected Outcome Component

The expected outcome completes the job statement: "...so I can [expected outcome]."

The expected outcome is what becomes possible when the job is done. It is the life change the customer is making progress toward. It often reveals the emotional or social dimension more clearly than the motivation component.

**Questions to identify the expected outcome:**
- What did customers imagine would be different if the switch worked?
- What would they be able to do (or stop doing) that they can't (or have to) now?
- What feeling or state are they moving toward?
- What would success look like to them in concrete terms?

**Expected outcome examples — good vs. weak:**

| Weak Expected Outcome | Strong Expected Outcome |
|----------------------|------------------------|
| "so I can be more productive" | "so I can stop spending my Sundays catching up and actually rest" |
| "so I can save money" | "so I can stop feeling anxious about whether I have enough for what's coming" |
| "so I can work better with my team" | "so I can trust that everyone knows what they're responsible for and I don't have to chase people" |
| "so I can be organized" | "so I can find anything I need in 30 seconds instead of digging through email" |

**Draft the expected outcome component:**

```
EXPECTED OUTCOME DRAFTS

From interview data — what customers imagined would change:
  - "_______________"
  - "_______________"

Version 1: "so I can _______________"
Version 2: "so I can _______________"
Version 3: "so I can _______________"

Test: Is this the outcome, not another step in the process?
Does this reveal what the customer's life would be like when the job is done?
Selected: _______________
```

---

## Step 5: Assemble and Test the Full Job Statement

Combine the three components into the full statement and test it:

```
DRAFT JOB STATEMENT:

"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

Full statement: _______________
```

**Test 1: The Customer Recognition Test**
If you showed this statement to 5 customers who fit the profile, would at least 4 of them immediately say "yes, that's exactly it"? If they'd need to qualify it significantly, the statement is either too abstract or not capturing the right job.

**Test 2: The Specificity Test**
Does this statement distinguish the job from adjacent but different jobs? "I want to communicate clearly" is too broad. "I want to make sure my message lands the way I intend it when there's emotional risk in the conversation" is specific.

**Test 3: The Non-Product Test**
Could the job be done by multiple different types of solutions, not just your product? A good job statement is product-agnostic. If the statement only applies to your product, you've described a feature, not a job.

**Test 4: The Stability Test**
Is this job stable over time — will customers have this job regardless of what specific products exist to serve it? Jobs don't change much even as solutions change. "I want to hire a tool with X feature" is not a stable job.

**Test 5: The Dimension Test**
Does the statement capture a functional move, an emotional move, or a social move? A complete job usually has all three, though one typically dominates.

## Step 6: Document the Three Dimensions

Every job has functional, emotional, and social dimensions. The statement captures the dominant dimension but the full picture requires documenting all three.

**Functional Dimension**
The practical, objective task the customer is trying to accomplish. This is what they'd describe if asked "what are you trying to do?"

```
FUNCTIONAL DIMENSION:
What are they literally trying to accomplish? _______________
What does success look like in tangible terms? _______________
What process are they trying to execute or improve? _______________
```

**Emotional Dimension**
The feeling state the customer is trying to reach (or escape). Often hidden in the expected outcome. Customers are sometimes reluctant to voice this but it drives the switch.

```
EMOTIONAL DIMENSION:
What feeling are they trying to move toward? _______________
What feeling are they trying to escape? _______________
Key emotional quote from interviews: "_______________"
Is the emotional job being served by current solutions? [Yes / No / Poorly]
```

**Social Dimension**
How the customer wants to be perceived by others — or how they perceive themselves — as a result of the job being done. Often invisible but powerful, especially in workplace contexts.

```
SOCIAL DIMENSION:
How do they want to be seen by others when the job is done? _______________
What story does this switch tell about who they are? _______________
Is there a status or identity component? _______________
Key social quote from interviews: "_______________"
```

## Step 7: Validate Across Interviews

A job statement derived from one interview is a hypothesis. Validation requires checking it against multiple customers.

Use `AskUserQuestion` to review cross-interview evidence:

**Question:** "Does this job statement resonate across the customers you've interviewed, or does it only fit some of them?"

**Validation tests:**

```
CROSS-INTERVIEW VALIDATION

Interview 1 — Does the situation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the motivation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the expected outcome match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Overall fit: [Strong / Moderate / Weak]
              Notes: _______________

Interview 2 — Does the situation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the motivation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the expected outcome match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Overall fit: [Strong / Moderate / Weak]
              Notes: _______________

Interview 3 — Does the situation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the motivation match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Does the expected outcome match? [Yes / Partial / No]
              Overall fit: [Strong / Moderate / Weak]
              Notes: _______________

Validation result:
  [Strong match] — All or most customers clearly fit this job
  [Segmented] — Some customers fit; others may have a related but distinct job
  [Too broad] — Statement captures too many different jobs; needs narrowing
  [Too narrow] — Statement only fits one customer; needs broadening
```

**If segmented:** Draft separate job statements for each distinct segment and note what distinguishes them. Two customers may hire the same product for different jobs — that's important to know.

---

## Step 8: Final Job Statement

Refine based on validation and produce the final documented job statement:

```
FINAL JOB STATEMENT

Primary Statement:
"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

Full text: _______________

Dimensions:
  Functional: _______________
  Emotional: _______________
  Social: _______________

Validated against: [# of interviews]
Confidence level: [High / Medium — needs more interviews]

What this job is NOT (related but distinct jobs we ruled out):
  - _______________
  - _______________

Competing jobs (other jobs our product might be hired for):
  - _______________
  - _______________

How well our product currently does this job: [Well / Partially / Poorly]
Evidence: _______________

Biggest gap between the job and our current product:
  _______________
```

---

## Output

By the end of this workflow, you will have:

1. A validated job statement with all three dimensions documented
2. Cross-interview validation evidence
3. Related and competing jobs identified
4. A gap analysis between the job and your current product

## What Comes Next

- Use the job statement to evaluate your current product: does it do this job?
- Use the job statement to inform messaging: your marketing should speak to the struggling moment and the expected outcome, not just features
- Use the job statement as a filter for roadmap decisions: does this candidate feature serve the job?
- Map the demand timeline in [map-demand-timeline.md](map-demand-timeline.md) to understand how demand for this job builds and resolves

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Situation too broad** — "When I use the product" is not a situation. It's a tautology.
2. **Motivation is a feature** — "I want a better dashboard" is not a motivation. Ask one more "but why?"
3. **Expected outcome is another step** — "so I can see my data" is not an outcome. What becomes possible when they can see their data?
4. **Skipping the emotional and social dimensions** — These are often more predictive of switching than the functional dimension.
5. **Deriving the statement from one interview** — One customer's story is a hypothesis. You need convergence across at least three.
6. **Using the statement to justify features you already have** — The job statement should reveal what the product should do, not confirm what you've already built.
