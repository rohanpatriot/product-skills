# Switch Interview Workflow

I'll guide you through conducting a timeline interview with a customer who recently switched to (or away from) your product. The goal is to reconstruct the full causal story of their decision — from the first moment they sensed something wasn't working, to the moment they were satisfied or disappointed after making the switch.

## When to Use

- When you want to understand why a customer purchased your product
- When you want to understand why a customer churned or switched away
- When you're trying to identify the struggling moment that triggered the decision
- When you need to understand what forces were acting on the customer during the switch
- When you're building a demand timeline for a segment

## Step 1: Prepare the Interview

Use `AskUserQuestion` to gather context before you design the interview:

**Question:** "Tell me about the switch you're investigating. What did the customer switch from, and what did they switch to?"

**Follow-ups:**
- When did the switch happen? (Recency matters — ideally within the past 3-6 months)
- Is this a new customer (switched to you) or a churned customer (switched away)?
- What do you already believe caused the switch?

Document your assumptions. These are what the interview will confirm, complicate, or contradict.

### Recruiting the Right Switchers

Not all customers make useful interview subjects. Interview customers who:

1. **Recently switched** — The memory is fresh. Timeline details decay fast.
2. **Made an active decision** — They considered alternatives. Passive customers (renewed by default, inherited the product) won't have a rich story.
3. **Had friction in the decision** — The more they deliberated, the more forces you can uncover.
4. **Represent a segment you want to understand** — Their story should generalize, even if only partially.

Avoid:
- Customers who switched for entirely external reasons (company policy, budget mandate)
- Customers who can't remember the decision or describe it in vague terms
- Customers who are currently unhappy — they'll give you a complaint list, not a causal story

### Interview Setup

- 60 minutes minimum. 90 minutes preferred for a full timeline.
- Record with permission. You will miss things in the moment.
- Set up in a comfortable, low-stakes environment. Not a formal conference room.
- One interviewer. One note-taker. Don't let note-taking fragment your attention.
- Open with a genuine, curious framing — not a survey framing.

Opening script:

> "I'm not here to sell you anything or evaluate whether you made the right decision. I'm genuinely curious about what was happening in your life when you decided to make this change. I'm going to ask you to walk me through it almost like you're telling me a story. The details — even the ones that seem minor — are what I'm most interested in. There are no wrong answers."

## Step 2: Establish the Big Hire Moment

Before walking back through the timeline, anchor the interview at the moment of purchase.

**Ask:**
> "Tell me about the day you actually [purchased / signed up / switched]. Walk me through what that day looked like."

Probe:
- Where were you?
- Who else was involved?
- What did you do immediately before pulling the trigger?
- What were you feeling in that moment?
- Was there a specific event or conversation that happened that day?

This is the big hire — the moment the customer hired your product. It anchors the timeline and gives you a specific event to walk backward from.

**Annotate in your notes:**

```
BIG HIRE MOMENT
Date/context: _______________
Key detail: _______________
Emotional state: _______________
Who was involved: _______________
Immediate trigger: _______________
```

## Step 3: Walk the Timeline — First Thought

Now walk backward to the very beginning.

**Ask:**
> "Let's go back further. Before you ever thought about [switching / buying this / trying something new] — what was the first moment you remember thinking 'something about this isn't working'?"

This is the struggling moment — the first crack in the status quo. It may feel small to the customer. That's okay. The first thought is often a quiet, almost unconscious signal that accumulates over time.

Probe for the struggling moment:
- "What exactly happened in that moment?"
- "Had that happened before, or was it the first time?"
- "What did you do after that thought? Did you act on it right away?"
- "How long had that been bothering you?"

**What you're listening for:**
- A specific event (not a vague feeling)
- The context: where they were, what they were doing, who was with them
- Evidence of how long the frustration had been building
- Whether this was a new problem or a recurring one that finally tipped

If the customer gives you a vague answer ("I just wasn't happy"), probe for the specific moment:

> "You said you weren't happy. Can you take me back to a specific day or situation when you remember feeling that? What was actually happening?"

**Annotate:**

```
FIRST THOUGHT
Date/context: _______________
Triggering event: _______________
How long building: _______________
Customer quote: "_______________"
Push force identified: _______________
```

## Step 4: Passive Looking

After the first thought, most customers don't immediately act. They enter a phase of passive looking — noticing alternatives, filing away information, without actively searching.

**Ask:**
> "After that first thought, did you immediately start looking for alternatives, or did some time pass?"

If time passed:
> "What was that period like? Were you noticing things — articles, conversations, ads — that you weren't paying attention to before?"

Probe:
- "Did you talk to anyone about it during this time?"
- "Did you come across anything that made you think 'that might be better'?"
- "What were you telling yourself about why you hadn't made a change yet?"

**What you're listening for:**
- Evidence of passive attention — noticing relevant information without seeking it
- Pull forces beginning to build (they're filing away alternatives)
- Anxiety and habit forces keeping them in passive mode
- The narrative they told themselves to justify not acting yet

The passive looking phase reveals Habit and Anxiety holding the customer in place even after Push has started building. This is crucial for understanding why the switch didn't happen sooner.

**Annotate:**

```
PASSIVE LOOKING
Duration: _______________
What they noticed: _______________
Who they talked to: _______________
What held them back: _______________
Pull forces emerging: _______________
Anxiety/Habit forces: _______________
Customer quote: "_______________"
```

## Step 5: Active Looking

At some point, passive noticing becomes active searching. Something tips the customer from passive to active mode.

**Ask:**
> "At some point it sounds like you went from just noticing things to actually looking. When did that happen? What changed?"

Probe:
- "What specifically made you say 'okay, I'm actually going to look at this'?"
- "What did active looking look like for you? What did you do?"
- "What were you trying to figure out? What questions did you have?"
- "Who did you talk to? What did you read or watch?"
- "What alternatives did you consider?"

**What you're listening for:**
- The specific trigger that moved them from passive to active (often a second struggling moment, or a pull force becoming concrete)
- What questions they were trying to answer — these reveal anxieties
- What alternatives they evaluated and why they were or weren't compelling
- Social forces: who did they talk to, whose opinion mattered?

If the customer jumped straight from first thought to active looking without a passive phase, note that — some customers move faster and the gap between push and action is shorter.

**Annotate:**

```
ACTIVE LOOKING
Trigger for transition: _______________
What they searched/evaluated: _______________
Key questions they were trying to answer: _______________
People they consulted: _______________
Alternatives considered: _______________
Why alternatives were/weren't compelling: _______________
Customer quote: "_______________"
```

## Step 6: The Deciding Moment

The deciding moment is the last meaningful point before the switch. It may be a conversation, a comparison, a trial experience, or simply a moment of resolution.

**Ask:**
> "Walk me through the moment when you finally decided. What happened right before you said 'I'm going to do this'?"

Probe:
- "Was there a specific conversation, event, or experience that made it feel clear?"
- "Did something tip the balance — something that made you feel confident enough to act?"
- "What were you most worried about going in?"
- "Was there anything that almost stopped you?"

**What you're listening for:**
- The resolution of the dominant anxiety — what finally made the fear feel manageable?
- The pull force that became concrete enough to outweigh habit
- Evidence of social proof, trial experience, or information that reduced anxiety
- Anything that nearly stopped the switch — residual anxiety or habit

**Annotate:**

```
DECIDING MOMENT
What tipped the balance: _______________
What they were most worried about: _______________
What resolved the dominant anxiety: _______________
What almost stopped them: _______________
Customer quote: "_______________"
```

## Step 7: The Big Hire

Return to the purchase/switch moment with more depth now that you have context.

**Ask:**
> "So you [made the purchase / signed up / made the switch]. What did that moment feel like?"

Probe:
- "Was it a relief? Exciting? Nerve-wracking?"
- "Who else was involved or knew about it?"
- "Did you have any second thoughts immediately after?"

**What you're listening for:**
- The emotional character of the big hire — relief signals strong push, excitement signals strong pull
- Residual anxiety immediately post-purchase
- Whether the customer fully committed or was holding something in reserve

---

## Step 8: The Little Hire — First Use

The big hire gets the product in the door. The little hire is what happens every time they use it. The first use is often the most revealing.

**Ask:**
> "Tell me about the first time you actually used [the product] after the switch. What happened?"

Probe:
- "What were you trying to do?"
- "Did it do what you expected?"
- "Was there anything that surprised you — positively or negatively?"
- "What did you think in that moment about whether you'd made the right decision?"

**What you're listening for:**
- Whether the product delivered on the implicit promise of the big hire
- Early delight or early friction — both leave strong memories
- Whether the job was done in the first use
- Any renegotiation of expectations happening in real time

**Annotate:**

```
LITTLE HIRE (First Use)
What they tried to do: _______________
Whether it worked: _______________
Surprises: _______________
Early verdict: _______________
Customer quote: "_______________"
```

---

## Step 9: Satisfaction or Disappointment

Close the timeline by understanding the current state.

**Ask:**
> "Now that you've been using it for [time period] — how do you feel about the decision?"

Probe:
- "Did it do what you were hoping it would do?"
- "Is there anything about your old [product/situation] that you miss?"
- "If you were talking to someone in the same situation you were in before, what would you tell them?"

For churned customers, adapt:
> "When did you start thinking this wasn't working? Walk me through what that was like."

**What you're listening for:**
- Whether the job is actually getting done (satisfaction = job done)
- Residual pull toward the old solution (unresolved habit or unfired expectation)
- New jobs that emerged post-switch that the product is or isn't doing
- The language they'd use to recommend (or warn) someone in the same situation — this is gold for positioning

## Common Failure Modes

1. **Accepting vague answers** — "I wasn't happy" is not a struggling moment. Probe for the specific event.
2. **Skipping the passive looking phase** — This is where Anxiety and Habit are most visible. Don't rush past it.
3. **Asking about hypotheticals** — Stay in the past. "What did you actually do?" not "What would you do?"
4. **Leading the witness** — Your job is to hear their story, not confirm your hypothesis.
5. **Synthesizing during the interview** — Document first, synthesize after. Don't let your interpretation cut off their story.
6. **Missing the little hire** — The big hire is the purchase. The little hire is the job. Ask about first use.
