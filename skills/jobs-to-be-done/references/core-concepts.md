# Core Concepts

The foundational ideas of Jobs-to-be-Done as developed through Bob Moesta's demand-side thinking. These concepts are not abstractions — they are precise claims about how demand works, why customers switch, and what product teams consistently get wrong.

## Jobs as Progress

The most important reframe in JTBD is that a "job" is not a task, a goal, or a pain point. A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a specific circumstance.

This distinction has significant consequences:
- **Tasks** describe what people do (search for a restaurant, fill out a form)
- **Goals** describe end states they want to reach (be healthy, save money)
- **Pain points** describe what's frustrating (the app is slow, I can't find what I need)
- **Jobs** describe the progress someone is trying to make given a specific context (make a good impression on a first date without looking like I'm trying too hard)

Why does this matter? Because tasks, goals, and pain points can all be addressed in ways that don't actually serve the job. You can make the form faster (task improvement), you can make saving easier (goal support), you can make the app more responsive (pain point fix) — and still miss the underlying progress the person is trying to make.

Jobs are also different from needs. Needs are often stated at the wrong level of abstraction ("I need a better way to take notes") or the wrong domain ("I need more time"). A job is specific to a situation: "When I'm in a meeting where I need to make a decision but don't have all the information, I want to capture the open questions in a way that I'll actually act on later, so I can follow through without the decision falling through the cracks."

**The job is always about the customer's life, not your product.** Your product is a potential means to an end — one of many solutions that could, in principle, do the job.

### What Makes a Good Job Statement

A well-formed job has three components:
1. **Situation:** The specific circumstance in which the job arises
2. **Motivation:** The progress the customer is trying to make
3. **Expected outcome:** What becomes possible when the job is done

Format: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

The situation grounds the job in a real context. Without it, the job is too abstract to design for. The motivation identifies the move the customer is making — not the feature they want. The expected outcome describes the changed state, not another step in the process.

### Jobs Are Stable; Solutions Are Not

Jobs change very slowly. The job of "get from here to there without being rained on" has existed for millennia. The solutions have changed dramatically: horses, carriages, trains, cars, rideshares. Product teams that anchor their strategy on the stable job have a durable foundation. Teams that anchor on a solution build on sand.

When Kodak thought they were in the film business, they missed digital photography. When Blockbuster thought they were in the video rental business, they missed streaming. Both were serving stable jobs (capture memories, be entertained at home) but anchored on solutions that became obsolete.

## The Hiring and Firing Metaphor

Customers don't "use" or "adopt" products. They hire them for a job and fire them when the job isn't done well enough.

This metaphor is precise, not casual. When you hire someone, you:
- Have a specific job that needs doing
- Look for candidates who can do that job
- Evaluate fit between the job requirements and the candidate's abilities
- Make a hiring decision based on expected job performance
- Monitor whether the job actually gets done
- Fire them if the job consistently isn't done

Customers make exactly these moves. The "hiring" frame forces product teams to ask the right questions: What job is this customer trying to do? Does our product do that job better than the alternatives? What causes customers to "fire" us?

### The Competition Is Broader Than You Think

When you frame your product as competing against similar products, you miss most of your actual competition. Customers don't hire your product against other products in your category — they hire it (and other things) against the job.

The milkshake example from Clayton Christensen's research illustrates this: McDonald's found that milkshakes were being hired for a morning commute job (something to do with your hand, filling enough to last until lunch, consumed without mess). The competition for that job was not other milkshakes — it was bananas, bagels, and coffee. Understanding the job revealed a totally different competitive set and pointed toward specific product improvements (thicker, so it lasts longer; more interesting stuff inside, so the commute feels less boring).

If you ask "what job is this customer hiring our product to do?", you might discover your product competes with a spreadsheet, a phone call, a new hire, or doing nothing — not the product you thought you were competing against.

### The Firing Decision Is As Important As the Hiring Decision

Product teams focus on why customers buy. They should spend equal attention on why customers leave. The firing decision contains critical information: Which job isn't getting done? At what stage of use does the customer decide to fire the product? What is the struggling moment that builds up before they fire?

Customers who fire your product often hire it again in a different context (they may be a different customer), or they hire a competitor, or they go back to the previous solution. In each case, their story is a data point about job performance.

## Demand-Side vs. Supply-Side Thinking

Most product development starts supply-side: we have a product, a technology, a capability, a team — what can we build? Supply-side thinking is about what we can make and sell.

Demand-side thinking starts from the customer and asks: what progress is this person trying to make, and what would help them make it? Demand-side is about understanding what customers are trying to hire before you design what to offer.

### The Asymmetry

Supply-side thinking produces products that companies think people need. Demand-side thinking produces products that people are already trying to hire — but can't find, or are hiring imperfectly.

This asymmetry explains why many product improvements fail: they improve what the company is already good at making (supply) rather than what customers are struggling to get done (demand). The company builds a better version of something nobody was trying to hire.

### How to Practice Demand-Side Thinking

Demand-side thinking requires starting every analysis with a causal question: "What was happening in this customer's life that caused them to seek a solution?" Not "what features do customers want?" but "what progress were they trying to make, and why couldn't they make it with what already existed?"

This means:
- Conducting switch interviews to understand the causal story of purchase decisions
- Looking for the struggling moment before looking for the solution
- Asking "who switched to us and why?" rather than "what features should we add?"
- Treating win/loss data as demand-side signal, not just competitive intelligence

Demand-side thinking also means accepting that not all customers want the same thing even if they look alike demographically. Two 35-year-old software engineers with similar incomes may be trying to make completely different progress with the same product category. Their demographics don't reveal their jobs.

## The Struggling Moment

The struggling moment is the specific event or experience that makes a customer say, for the first time or definitively, "something about this isn't working." It is the originating push force — the crack in the status quo that, over time, grows large enough to make switching feel worth the risk.

Understanding the struggling moment is critical because it is the actual cause of demand. Customers don't buy products because of features or price points or demographics. They buy products because something happened in their life that made the current situation feel intolerable enough to overcome the combined drag of Anxiety and Habit.

### Characteristics of a Good Struggling Moment

A well-identified struggling moment is:
- **Specific:** A particular event, not a vague feeling. "The third time the report crashed and I had to start over" not "I was frustrated with the reporting tool."
- **Dated:** It happened at a specific time. The customer can usually place it even if they don't remember the exact date.
- **Recurring or escalating:** Often the struggling moment is the last in a series of smaller incidents. "This has happened before, but this time..."
- **Emotionally loaded:** The customer uses emotional language: "I was so fed up", "I couldn't take it anymore", "I remember thinking this has to change."

### Why the Struggling Moment Is the Starting Point

If you understand what the struggling moment is for your customers, you know:
- What to say in marketing (speak to the moment before they found you)
- What triggers to look for in product analytics (events that correlate with the struggling moment)
- What the job is (the struggling moment often points directly at the functional job)
- Where to look for new customers (people who are currently experiencing this moment)

Many product teams describe their customers by what features they use. JTBD teams describe their customers by the struggling moment that brought them there.

## Big Hire vs. Little Hire

There are two hiring decisions in every customer relationship, and they are not the same decision.

### The Big Hire

The big hire is the moment of purchase — when the customer brings the product into their life and says "I'm going to give this a try." The big hire is driven by:
- The accumulated Push forces making the current situation intolerable
- The Pull forces creating a compelling vision of the better state
- The resolution of the dominant Anxiety
- The weakening of Habit

The big hire is a hopeful act. The customer is hiring your product with an expectation of what progress it will help them make. That expectation is real and specific, even if the customer hasn't articulated it explicitly.

### The Little Hire

The little hire happens every time the customer actually uses the product. It is the moment they pick up the product and put it to work for the job. Every little hire is a fresh decision: is this worth using right now?

Many products survive the big hire but fail at the little hire. The customer bought the gym membership (big hire) but never developed the habit of going (little hire fails repeatedly). The customer installed the app (big hire) but doesn't open it after the first week (little hires declining). The customer signed up for the service (big hire) but routes around it for the actual work (little hire substituted by the old solution).

### Why Both Matter

The big hire is won by marketing, sales, and the product's reputation. The little hire is won by the product's ability to actually do the job in the moment of use.

Optimizing for big hire (acquisition) without understanding little hire (engagement) produces a leaky bucket. Customers acquire, fail at little hire, and leave — often without ever telling you why.

### Diagnosing Big vs. Little Hire Failures

**Big hire failing:** Low acquisition or high early churn immediately after purchase.
Likely causes: Anxiety not resolved (product not credible), Pull not compelling (vision of better state unclear), Push insufficient in target segment (they're not that frustrated).

**Little hire failing:** Acquisition seems fine but engagement drops and customers eventually leave.
Likely causes: Job not actually being done in daily use, product requires too much behavior change (Habit not overcome), unexpected friction surfaces after the big hire.

## Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

Every job has three dimensions that operate simultaneously. Most product teams see only the functional dimension and miss the deeper drivers of customer behavior.

### Functional Jobs

The functional dimension is what the customer is literally trying to accomplish — the practical, objective task. "Get from A to B." "Process these invoices." "Communicate this decision to the team."

Functional jobs are the easiest to identify because customers can describe them directly. They're also the easiest to copy and commoditize. If your product only serves the functional job, you're in a feature war.

Product improvement driven purely by functional analysis tends to produce incrementally better solutions to the same task. It misses the emotional and social dimensions that often drive the actual switching decision.

### Emotional Jobs

The emotional dimension is the feeling state the customer is trying to reach or escape. This is rarely stated explicitly in interviews — you hear it in the language around the functional job.

- "I want to feel like I'm in control of this"
- "I need to stop being anxious about whether this is going to work"
- "I want to feel confident when I walk into that meeting"
- "I don't want to feel embarrassed in front of my team"

Emotional jobs explain why two products that do the same functional job are experienced completely differently. A to-do list app and a project management suite might handle the same functional job (track tasks) but serve completely different emotional jobs (feel on top of things vs. look organized and in control to leadership).

When a customer says a product "feels right" or describes relief after switching, they're describing an emotional job being done.

### Social Jobs

The social dimension is how the customer wants to be perceived by others — or how they see themselves — as a result of using the product. Social jobs are powerful and often invisible.

- "I want my team to see me as someone who has things under control"
- "I want to feel like the kind of person who makes informed decisions"
- "I want my manager to trust that I'll catch things before they become problems"
- "I want to be the person who found this tool before everyone else"

Social jobs explain many professional tool purchases that look irrational from a purely functional perspective. Why do companies pay for premium analytics platforms when cheaper alternatives exist? Often because the social job ("be seen as a data-driven organization") is being done in addition to the functional job.

### Working with All Three Dimensions

Identify all three dimensions in your job statement. A well-designed product does the functional job, contributes to the emotional job, and serves the social job. A product that only does the functional job is vulnerable to competition from products that also address the emotional and social dimensions.

## The Job vs. The Solution

The most important distinction in JTBD practice is between the job and the solution. Products, features, and services are solutions. The job is what the customer is trying to do before any particular solution exists.

This distinction is not semantic. It is structural. Conflating the job with the solution causes teams to:
- Build features that serve the product instead of the job
- Miss competitive threats from outside their product category
- Optimize existing solutions instead of looking for better ways to do the job
- Narrow their market to people who want this type of solution rather than people who have this job

### The Test

If you're not sure whether something is a job or a solution, ask: "Could a different type of product do this?" If yes — if the progress could be made through a different means — it's a job. If no — if only this type of product can do it — you're describing a feature or a solution.

"Automate my expense reports" is a solution description. "Make sure I never have to think about expense tracking again" is closer to a job. "Have full confidence that I'm compliant and not leaving money on the table without spending any attention on it" is the job.

### Implications for Product Strategy

If you define your business as delivering a solution, your strategy is defensive (how do we make our solution better?). If you define your business as serving a job, your strategy is offensive (what is the best possible way to do this job, regardless of what it requires?). The second question opens up more territory and leads to more durable competitive advantage.

Intuit didn't succeed by building better accounting software. They succeeded by understanding the job of "make me feel in control of my money without having to become a financial expert" — and that job guided their product decisions for decades.
