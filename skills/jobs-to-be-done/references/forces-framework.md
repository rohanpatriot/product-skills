# Forces Framework

A deep dive on the four forces of progress — Push, Pull, Anxiety, and Habit — how they interact, what dominance patterns mean, and how to design products and go-to-market motions that work with the forces rather than against them.

## The Four Forces Model

Every switching decision is shaped by four forces acting simultaneously on the customer:

```
                    SUPPRESSING FORCES
           ┌─────────────────────────────┐
           │     Anxiety    │   Habit    │
           │  (fear of new) │ (comfort   │
           │                │  of old)   │
           └────────────────┴────────────┘
                            ↑
                     blocks the switch
                            |
Push ──────────────────────────────────────── Pull
(frustration with old)                 (attraction to new)
                            |
                     drives the switch
                            ↓
           ┌────────────────┬────────────┐
           │  Push          │    Pull    │
           │ (drives away   │ (draws     │
           │  from current) │  toward    │
           │                │  new)      │
           └─────────────────────────────┘
                    GENERATING FORCES
```

A switch happens when the generating forces (Push + Pull) exceed the suppressing forces (Anxiety + Habit).

This model is causal, not descriptive. It explains why the switch happened or didn't happen — not just what happened. It tells you which forces to amplify and which to reduce in your product and go-to-market strategy.

## Push: The Force of Frustration

Push is the internal pressure driving a customer away from their current situation. It is the accumulation of frustrations, failures, and unmet expectations that makes the status quo feel intolerable.

### What Push Is and Isn't

Push is NOT:
- A feature request ("I wish the product had X")
- A preference ("I like Y better")
- An abstract dissatisfaction ("I'm not happy with the product")

Push IS:
- A specific, recurring experience of failure or frustration
- An emotional charge that builds over time
- The "this has to change" moment

Push is always situated. "The app keeps crashing when I'm trying to close a deal on my phone" is Push. "The app is sometimes unstable" is not — it's too vague to be causal.

### How Push Builds

Push rarely appears fully formed. It accumulates through a series of incidents:

1. **First incident:** Minor frustration, noted but not acted on. "That's annoying."
2. **Repeated incidents:** The same friction appears again. "This is a pattern."
3. **Consequential incident:** The frustration costs the customer something meaningful — time, money, a relationship, an opportunity. "This is costing me."
4. **Struggling moment:** The customer says "enough." The status quo is no longer acceptable.

The struggling moment is the peak of Push — the point at which the accumulated frustration tips into active searching.

### Push Varies by Situation

The same product feature can produce high Push in one customer situation and zero Push in another. A slow invoice approval process is a minor inconvenience to a business owner who invoices twice a year and a career-defining problem for a finance manager whose bonus depends on DSO. Understanding Push means understanding the specific situation — not the feature complaint in the abstract.

### Identifying Push in Interviews

Listen for:
- The language of accumulation: "I kept running into...", "Every time I tried to...", "It kept happening..."
- The language of cost: "It was costing us...", "We lost...", "I had to...", "It took..."
- The language of emotion: "I was so fed up", "I couldn't believe", "I was done with it"
- Specific incidents with dates, contexts, and consequences — not vague descriptions

Push probing questions:
- "What wasn't working about the old situation?"
- "When was the moment you finally said 'I have to change this'?"
- "What was the cost of staying as you were?"
- "Was this a new problem or had it been building?"

### Push in Product and Marketing

Marketing that addresses Push speaks directly to the struggling moment: "Tired of [specific frustration]?" It validates that the customer's experience is real and common. It doesn't invent a problem — it reflects a problem back at customers who are already living it.

If your marketing doesn't resonate, one of two things is true: either you've identified the wrong frustration (you're speaking to a problem that isn't the real Push) or your segment doesn't have strong enough Push to be in the market (they're tolerating the status quo).

## Pull: The Force of Attraction

Pull is the attraction of a compelling vision of a better future that draws the customer toward making the switch. Pull is not about features — it is about progress. What does the customer imagine their life or work will look like if the switch works?

### What Pull Is and Isn't

Pull is NOT:
- A feature list ("Your product has X, Y, and Z")
- A comparative advantage ("You're better than the competitor at...")
- Social proof alone ("Other people use it")

Pull IS:
- A concrete vision of the better state ("I could finally stop worrying about...")
- An aspiration the customer identifies with ("I'd be the kind of person who...")
- A specific imagined experience ("I could see how this would work for me when...")

Pull is personal. It is the customer's own imagination of the better future — not your description of your product. Your marketing can stimulate Pull by helping customers imagine the better state, but Pull is not something you can force. It has to connect with something the customer already wants.

### How Pull Builds

Pull often starts during the passive looking phase, when the customer is noticing relevant information without seeking it. A word-of-mouth recommendation, a case study, a demo seen in passing — these plant the seed of the better state.

Pull becomes concrete during active looking, when the customer tries the product, sees a demonstration, or reads a specific success story that makes the better state feel real and achievable. This is the moment Pull becomes strong enough to sustain action against Anxiety.

### The Difference Between Push-Driven and Pull-Driven Switches

**Push-driven switch:** The customer is fleeing a problem more than pursuing a vision. They chose you because you were better than what they had, not because they had a compelling vision of what you'd deliver. These customers often have moderate satisfaction — you solved their problem, but they don't feel strongly about you as a brand or product.

**Pull-driven switch:** The customer had a vision of the better state and deliberately sought you out. They have higher expectations but also higher engagement and loyalty. They feel the switch more fully.

Most real switches involve both Push and Pull. The question is which dominates — because the dominant force shapes the customer's expectations and the nature of their engagement.

### Identifying Pull in Interviews

Listen for:
- The language of vision: "I imagined I could finally...", "I thought this could solve...", "I could see how..."
- The language of attraction: "I got excited about...", "I thought that was amazing...", "I had to try it"
- The specific trigger: a recommendation, a demo, an article, a competitor's use of the product
- The moment Pull became concrete: when did the vision move from abstract to felt as real?

Pull probing questions:
- "What first caught your attention about [new solution]?"
- "What did you imagine your [work/life] would look like if this worked?"
- "Was there a specific moment when you thought 'this might actually be the thing'?"
- "When you were in active looking mode, what were you hoping to find?"

### Pull in Product and Marketing

Marketing that creates Pull shows customers the vision of the better state — in their specific situation, with their specific job. Not features. Not comparisons. The imagined future.

Product teams can create Pull by making the vision of the better state tangible early in the customer journey: during trial, in onboarding, in the first use of the product. The little hire either delivers on the implicit promise of the big hire (reinforcing Pull into satisfaction) or breaks it (Pull collapses into disappointment).

## Anxiety: The Force of Resistance

Anxiety is the fear of making the wrong choice — the perceived risk of switching — that holds customers back from acting even when Push and Pull are both present.

### What Anxiety Is and Isn't

Anxiety is NOT irrational. Customers who are anxious have real things to lose: investments in the current solution, workflow dependencies, relationships, professional credibility. They are doing rational risk assessment.

Anxiety IS:
- Specific concerns about what could go wrong
- Fear of the unknown in the new solution
- Worry about the cost of being wrong
- Concern about what others will think

### Types of Anxiety

**Performance anxiety:** "What if the new product doesn't actually work for my situation?" This is the most basic anxiety: will it do the job? This is addressed by trials, demos, specific case studies, and reference customers in similar situations.

**Switching cost anxiety:** "What about everything I've built in the current system?" Data, configurations, workflows, integrations — switching means leaving these behind or recreating them. Addressed by migration tools, import capabilities, and "bring your data" features.

**Social anxiety:** "What will my team/boss/peers think if this doesn't work out?" The professional risk of making a decision that fails publicly. Addressed by social proof from credible peers, phased rollouts that reduce risk visibility, and framing that shares responsibility ("many teams like yours use...").

**Learning anxiety:** "What if it's too complicated and I can't figure it out?" Addressed by clean onboarding, in-context help, and quick-win design that shows early success.

**Buyer's remorse anxiety:** "What if I pay for this and regret it?" Addressed by free trials, money-back guarantees, phased pricing (start small), and exit ramps that make it easy to cancel if it doesn't work.

**Timing anxiety:** "Is this the right time? What if something better comes out?" Addressed by demonstrating the current cost of waiting and the compounding benefit of starting now.

### How Anxiety Manifests in Interviews

High-Anxiety customers have long passive looking phases. They describe elaborate evaluation processes. They consulted many people. They sought out trials and references. They can articulate specific fears. They describe relief at the deciding moment.

Low-Anxiety customers (or customers whose Anxiety was quickly resolved) moved faster from first thought to big hire. They describe less deliberation and fewer concerns.

### Anxiety Probing Questions

- "What were you most worried about when you were thinking about making this switch?"
- "What almost stopped you from going through with it?"
- "What did you imagine could go wrong?"
- "Was there anyone who warned you against doing this?"
- "What did you do to try to reduce your risk before committing?"
- "When did you feel like it was going to be okay to go ahead?"

## Habit: The Force of Inertia

Habit is the comfort of the familiar — the inertia that keeps customers with their current solution even when they are frustrated with it. Habit is not stupidity; it is optimization. The current solution is known. Switching to an unknown is risky. Staying is the path of least resistance.

### What Habit Is and Isn't

Habit is NOT:
- Satisfaction with the current solution (customers can be habitual and unsatisfied simultaneously)
- Active preference for the current solution
- A cognitive or emotional failure

Habit IS:
- The accumulated switching cost of leaving the current solution
- The comfort of a known workflow
- The cognitive overhead of learning something new
- The relational investment in the current solution (support relationships, communities, teams)
- The identity connection to "how I do things"

### Types of Habit

**Workflow habit:** "I've built everything around how this works." The customer's daily process is designed around the current solution. Switching means redesigning the workflow, which is costly and disruptive.

**Skill habit:** "I know how to use this. I'd have to learn something new." The customer has invested time developing skill with the current solution. That investment feels like it would be lost in a switch.

**Relationship habit:** "I know the people here." Account managers, support teams, communities — these are social investments that don't transfer.

**Integration habit:** "Everything connects to this." Technical dependencies — APIs, data pipelines, connected tools — make switching technically costly.

**Identity habit:** "This is just how I do things." The deepest form of habit: the current solution is part of how the customer defines themselves professionally. "I'm a [tool] person." Switching means not just changing tools but changing identity.

### How Habit Manifests in Interviews

High-Habit customers have long passive looking phases even when Push is significant. They describe things they genuinely liked about the old solution. They emphasize the things they lost or had to rebuild. They describe a gradual transition rather than a hard switch. They may still occasionally default to the old solution for familiar tasks.

### Habit Probing Questions

- "Even after you started noticing this wasn't working, what kept you where you were for a while?"
- "What was still working fine about the old situation?"
- "What would you have lost if you'd switched earlier?"
- "Was there anything about the old solution that you'd genuinely miss?"
- "What was the story you told yourself about why it was okay to stay?"
- "How long did you run the old solution in parallel before fully switching?"

## How the Four Forces Interact

The forces don't operate in sequence — they act simultaneously, throughout the decision process.

### The Basic Equation

Switch occurs when: Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit

But this equation is not static. The forces shift across the timeline:

- **First thought through passive looking:** Push is building. Anxiety and Habit are suppressing any action. Pull is beginning to emerge in the form of noticing alternatives.
- **Active looking:** Push has tipped into action. Pull is becoming concrete. Anxiety is being actively assessed and reduced. Habit is starting to loosen.
- **Deciding moment:** One force resolves or tips. Often Anxiety reaches resolution ("I've done enough due diligence to feel okay") or Pull becomes vivid enough to outweigh residual Anxiety and Habit.
- **Post-purchase:** Push and Habit are no longer active. Pull is being tested against reality. A new Anxiety may emerge (buyer's remorse). If the product delivers, Pull transitions into satisfaction.

### Force Interactions

**Push amplifies Pull:** When customers are frustrated enough, their vision of the better state becomes more vivid and compelling. High Push makes Pull feel more urgent.

**Anxiety suppresses Pull:** Even with a compelling vision of the better state, Anxiety keeps customers in passive mode. "I can see how that would be great, but I'm not sure it will work for us."

**Habit suppresses Push:** When Habit is strong, customers tolerate more frustration before acting. They have more invested in the current solution, so the switching cost feels higher.

**Resolved Anxiety unlocks the switch:** The deciding moment often happens when the dominant Anxiety reaches resolution — through a trial, a conversation, a case study, a guarantee. The removal of the primary fear tips the balance.

## Force Dominance Patterns

In every switching decision, one force tends to dominate — meaning if that force were different, the switch would have happened differently or not at all.

### Pattern 1: Push-Dominant

**Characteristics:** Strong Push (specific, accumulated, consequential frustrations) driving an escape more than a pursuit. Pull present but not the primary driver. Anxiety and Habit overcome by sheer force of frustration.

**What it means for the customer:** They switched to escape, not to achieve. They have modest expectations — "anything better than what I had." They may not feel strongly loyal to you; they just feel strongly opposed to going back.

**Implications:**
- **Product:** Focus on reliability and not creating new frustrations. This customer switched away from a product that let them down; don't let them down again.
- **Marketing:** Validate the frustration. Speak to the struggling moment. "Tired of [specific frustration]?" resonates.
- **Risk:** If you create new frustrations, this customer's Push will rebuild against you.

### Pattern 2: Pull-Dominant

**Characteristics:** Moderate Push, strong Pull. Customer was attracted to the vision of the better state more than driven away from the current situation. May have been satisfied enough with the old solution.

**What it means for the customer:** They have a clear vision of what success looks like with you. High expectations. More likely to be disappointed if the product doesn't live up to the imagined state.

**Implications:**
- **Product:** The first use must deliver on the vision. The little hire must match the big hire promise.
- **Marketing:** Continue painting the vision of the better state. Don't switch to feature marketing after the sale.
- **Onboarding:** Show them the specific outcome they imagined as quickly as possible.

### Pattern 3: Anxiety-Blocked

**Characteristics:** Moderate to high Push and Pull, but the customer almost didn't switch — or switched very late — because Anxiety dominated. Long evaluation, many conversations, extensive due diligence.

**What it means for the customer:** They were ready to switch but had significant fear of being wrong. The decision required a lot of work to de-risk. They feel cautious about the product even after the big hire.

**Implications:**
- **Product:** Trials, free tiers, easy migration, and a generous cancellation policy directly address this pattern.
- **Onboarding:** Early wins are critical — they need quick confirmation that the switch was right.
- **Marketing:** Social proof from credible similar customers, case studies with specific outcomes, guarantees.

### Pattern 4: Habit-Blocked

**Characteristics:** Push and Pull both present, but the customer spent a long time in passive mode due to strong Habit. The switch eventually happened but was slow. Likely ran old and new solutions in parallel for some time.

**What it means for the customer:** They had significant investment in the current solution. Switching was a real disruption. They may still default to old behaviors in certain contexts.

**Implications:**
- **Product:** Migration tools, templates, import capabilities, and onboarding that maps to their existing workflow.
- **Onboarding:** Don't ask customers to abandon everything at once. Identify the one job to start with and build from there.
- **Marketing:** Reduce switching cost language: "Get started in 10 minutes", "Bring your existing data", "Keep using your current tools while you evaluate."

## Designing to Amplify Push and Pull

The best product and marketing strategies work with the forces rather than against them.

### Amplifying Push

**In marketing:** Speak to the struggling moment. Describe the specific frustrations your customers are living with. Mirror their language back at them. If they describe the problem as "I never know where anything stands," use that phrase — not "improve visibility" or "enhance transparency."

**In product:** Surfaces and metrics that show customers the cost of their current situation. Free trials that let customers experience how much better things could be. "Before/after" comparisons that make the current frustration tangible.

**In sales:** Help prospects articulate their struggling moment to themselves. The salesperson who can name the customer's frustration more precisely than the customer can builds immediate trust and credibility.

### Amplifying Pull

**In marketing:** Show the vision of the better state in the customer's specific context. Not generic success stories — success stories that match the segment you're targeting. Before/after narratives. Specific outcomes with numbers.

**In product:** The first use experience must deliver a taste of the vision. Design the onboarding around the job the customer hired you for. Make the first win happen fast.

**In sales:** Help prospects imagine their specific better state. "What would change for you if you didn't have to [current frustration] anymore?" "What would you do with that time?"

## Designing to Reduce Anxiety and Habit

### Reducing Anxiety

**Performance anxiety:** Free trials, sandbox environments, reference customers in similar situations, case studies with measurable outcomes, product guarantees.

**Switching cost anxiety:** Import tools, migration support, templates and starting points, parallel running capabilities, "try it alongside your current tool" framing.

**Social anxiety:** "Join X other teams like yours", customer logos in relevant industries, phased rollouts that start small, shared decision-making tools that spread responsibility.

**Learning anxiety:** In-context help, onboarding that produces a win in under 10 minutes, "get started in 5 minutes" messaging, support that feels human and accessible.

**Buyer's remorse anxiety:** Money-back guarantees, month-to-month pricing, cancellation without friction, no lock-in language.

### Reducing Habit

Habit reduction is fundamentally about reducing switching cost. The more you can make "switching" feel like adding rather than replacing, the lower the Habit force.

**Design strategies:**
- Import existing data and workflows on day one
- Map onboarding to the customer's existing process, not an idealized workflow
- Identify the one job to start with — don't ask customers to change everything at once
- Create a "quick win" that proves value without full commitment
- Enable parallel running during evaluation (old and new simultaneously)
- Build integrations with the tools they're already using so the switch feels additive, not subtractive

**Messaging strategies:**
- "Keep using [existing tool] while you evaluate" reduces perceived commitment
- "Get started with just [one use case]" reduces scope anxiety
- "Bring everything you already have" addresses data abandonment fear
- "No credit card required" reduces financial commitment anxiety early

## Using Forces to Diagnose Product Failures

When a product isn't growing, retaining customers, or converting trials — the forces framework provides a diagnostic structure.

### Diagnosis: Low Acquisition (Big Hire Not Happening)

Run the forces equation: Push + Pull < Anxiety + Habit

Ask:
- **Is Push too low?** Are customers not frustrated enough with the status quo? Is the struggling moment we're targeting actually occurring for our segment? Are we targeting customers who don't have the job, or who have it at low intensity?
- **Is Pull too weak?** Is our vision of the better state not compelling enough? Are we describing features instead of the better state? Is our vision too abstract to feel real?
- **Is Anxiety too high?** Are customers afraid of switching in ways we're not addressing? Is the product too complex, too expensive, too risky? Are our trials not de-risking effectively?
- **Is Habit too strong?** Are customers too invested in the current solution? Is the switching cost too high? Do we not have migration/import tools? Are we asking for too big a commitment upfront?

### Diagnosis: Low Retention (Little Hire Failing)

Little hire failure means the job isn't being done in daily use. Ask:
- Did we misidentify the job? Are we designing for the wrong progress?
- Is the product doing the functional job but failing the emotional or social job?
- Is the first use experience too far from the expectation set at big hire?
- Is there a specific friction point in daily use that creates new Push (pushing customers away from us)?
- Are customers reverting to old habits because the little hire requires too much behavior change?

### Diagnosis: High Anxiety in Trials (Trial to Purchase Failing)

If customers are using trials but not converting to purchase:
- Map the specific anxieties preventing conversion
- What questions are trial users asking before they're willing to commit?
- Is there a specific feature or capability gap that's fueling performance anxiety?
- Is pricing structure creating buyer's remorse anxiety?
- Is social anxiety blocking individual champions from getting organizational buy-in?

Each diagnosis leads to a specific intervention — not a generic improvement roadmap, but a targeted response to the specific force that's creating the problem.
