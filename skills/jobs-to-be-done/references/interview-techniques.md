## The Documentary Filmmaker Mindset

The single most useful reframe for JTBD interviewing is to think of yourself as a documentary filmmaker, not a researcher.

A researcher has hypotheses to confirm or deny. A filmmaker is curious about what actually happened — the full story, the unexpected details, the emotional moments — and wants to capture it completely before interpreting it.

This mindset changes how you conduct the interview:
- You are not testing a theory. You are reconstructing a story.
- There are no wrong answers. Everything the customer says is data about their experience.
- Your job is to keep the story moving forward (or backward through the timeline), not to evaluate what you're hearing.
- You want specifics, not opinions. You want events, not generalizations.

**What the filmmaker mindset sounds like in practice:**

"Walk me through what happened."
"Tell me more about that moment."
"What did you actually do next?"
"Who was in the room when that happened?"
"What were you feeling right then?"

What it does NOT sound like:

"So that was a pain point for you?"
"Would you say that was the main reason?"
"Did you feel like [product X] could have done that better?"

The filmmaker captures. The filmmaker does not editorialize. The filmmaker does not suggest. The filmmaker just keeps asking "and then what happened?"

## Recruiting the Right Switchers

Recruiting for JTBD research is different from recruiting for usability or preference research. You are looking for people who made a deliberate, emotionally engaged decision — not people who happened to use a product.

### Who to Recruit

**Recent switchers:** Interview people who made the switch in the past 3-6 months. Memory is fresh enough for timeline detail but the emotional dust has settled enough for reflection.

**Active decision-makers:** Find people who made a choice — they evaluated alternatives, felt the friction of the decision. Passive acquirers (inherited a product, followed a mandate) won't have a rich story.

**High-friction customers:** The most useful interviews come from customers who took a long time to decide, considered multiple alternatives, or were clearly deliberating. Their story has more force detail.

**The right direction of switch:** Be clear on whether you want people who switched TO you (acquisition understanding) or away FROM you (churn understanding). Both are valuable; they answer different questions.

### Recruiting Language

Don't frame the recruitment around your product or your goals. Frame it around their experience:

Good framing: "I'm trying to understand what it was like for you when you were considering [category change]. This isn't a sales call — I genuinely want to hear your story."

Poor framing: "We want to understand how customers discover and evaluate our product." (This sounds like a sales call or a marketing survey.)

### Where to Find Switchers

- **New customers (switched to you):** Identify customers who signed up in the past 3-6 months. Filter for those with clear activation signals (they actually used the product, not just signed up). Offer a simple incentive — $75-100 gift card works well for a 60-minute interview.
- **Churned customers (switched away):** This population is harder to reach because they've disengaged. Email at the point of cancellation with a genuine curiosity framing: "We're not trying to win you back — we just want to understand what happened. Your perspective would genuinely help us."
- **Competitor switchers (switched to a competitor):** This requires creative recruiting — LinkedIn, customer communities, or a recruiting firm. The investment is often worth it for the insight.

### How Many Interviews

You're looking for patterns, not statistical significance. As a rule:
- 5 interviews in the same segment will surface the primary patterns
- 10 interviews will give you confidence in those patterns and reveal secondary dynamics
- 15-20 interviews will produce diminishing returns in a well-defined segment

If patterns are still changing significantly after 8 interviews, you may be interviewing across multiple segments. Consider whether you have one job or multiple distinct jobs.

## Interview Setup and Environment

The physical and relational setup of the interview affects the quality of the data significantly.

### Format Options

**Remote video call:** Most accessible. Works well if you ask the customer to be somewhere private and comfortable (not a noisy open office). The slight distance of video can actually reduce pressure.

**In-person:** Higher quality for reading body language, energy, and nonverbal signals. Worth the investment for foundational research.

**The customer's home or workplace:** Best for capturing context clues — you see their actual environment, which sometimes reveals jobs that wouldn't come up in conversation.

### Setup Rules

**One interviewer, one note-taker:** Don't split your attention between tracking the story and capturing it. The note-taker watches for quotes, energy shifts, and things to follow up on. They can signal the interviewer with a note but don't interrupt.

**Sixty minutes minimum:** The timeline can't be done in 30 minutes without cutting stages. Budget 90 minutes if you want to go deep.

**Record with explicit permission:** "I'd like to record this conversation so I can review the details later — is that okay with you?" Recording is not optional; you will miss things.

**Open honestly:** Tell the customer what you're doing and why. Not marketing. Not sales. Genuinely curious about their experience. People who feel safe tell better stories.

**Start with rapport:** Spend the first 5 minutes building genuine connection before starting the timeline. Ask about something in their environment, their role, their day. A customer who is relaxed and talking freely gives much better data than one who is formal and careful.

### The Opening Frame

Script to adapt:

> "I'm going to ask you to walk me through your experience of [switching/deciding to buy/leaving]. I'm going to ask you a lot of questions that might feel basic or obvious — and I want you to answer them anyway, because the details are exactly what I'm looking for. There's no right answer. I'm not testing you. I'm genuinely trying to understand what it was like to be you going through this."

## Timeline Probing

The JTBD switch interview is structured as a timeline reconstruction. You are not asking for opinions, preferences, or evaluations. You are asking what actually happened at each stage. Here is the probing approach for each stage.

### First Thought Probing

Goal: Find the earliest signal that something wasn't working — the very first crack in the status quo.

Opener: "Let's go all the way back. Before you ever thought about making this change — what was the first moment you remember thinking 'something about this isn't working'?"

Follow-ups:
- "Had that happened before, or was that the first time?"
- "What did you actually do right after that moment?"
- "How long had this been bothering you? Was it a new thing or had it been building?"
- "What was the situation exactly? Where were you, what were you doing?"

Probes when the customer gives a vague first thought:
- "You said you weren't happy. Can you take me back to a specific day when you felt that? What was happening?"
- "What's the one thing you remember that made you think 'I need to do something about this'?"

### Passive Looking Probing

Goal: Understand the gap between first awareness and active searching — and what kept the customer in passive mode.

Opener: "After that first thought, did you immediately start looking for alternatives, or did some time pass?"

Follow-ups (if time passed):
- "What was that period like? Were you noticing things — articles, conversations, ads — that you weren't paying attention to before?"
- "What were you still getting out of [old solution] during that time? What was still working?"
- "What was the story you were telling yourself about why it was okay to stay?"
- "Who did you mention this to during that period?"

### Active Looking Probing

Goal: Understand what triggered active searching and what the customer did.

Opener: "At some point it sounds like you went from just noticing things to actually looking. When did that happen? What changed?"

Follow-ups:
- "What specifically made you say 'okay, I'm actually going to look at this'?"
- "What did active looking look like for you — what did you do?"
- "What were you trying to figure out? What were your main questions?"
- "Who did you talk to? What did you read?"
- "What alternatives did you seriously consider? What made them more or less compelling?"
- "Was there anything you saw that almost made you go with a different option?"

### Deciding Moment Probing

Goal: Find the resolution moment — what tipped the balance.

Opener: "Walk me through the moment when you finally decided. What happened right before you said 'I'm going to do this'?"

Follow-ups:
- "Was there a specific conversation, event, or experience that made it feel clear?"
- "What were you most worried about going in? Did that worry get resolved?"
- "Was there anything that almost stopped you?"
- "Did you talk to anyone before making the final decision? What did they say?"

### First Use Probing

Goal: Understand what happened in the critical early moments of little hire.

Opener: "Tell me about the first time you actually used [product] after the switch. What did you try to do?"

Follow-ups:
- "Did it work the way you expected?"
- "Was there anything that surprised you — positively or negatively?"
- "What did you think in that moment about whether you'd made the right call?"
- "Did you reach out to anyone after that first use? What did you tell them?"

## Energy Mapping

Energy refers to how actively engaged the customer is with the problem at each stage of the timeline. Tracking energy gives you structural information about the shape of the switching decision.

### How to Read Energy in the Conversation

Customers signal energy in the vocabulary they use:

**High energy signals:**
- Action verbs: "I called, I searched, I compared, I tested"
- Emotional intensity: "I was so frustrated, I couldn't stop thinking about it"
- Memory richness: Specific dates, names, locations — the memory is strong because the experience was vivid
- Length of response: They talk for 2-3 minutes without needing prompting

**Low energy signals:**
- Passive language: "I noticed, I thought about it, I figured I'd look eventually"
- Vague timeline: "I don't remember exactly when" or "sometime around then"
- Short responses with little detail
- Descriptions of staying put: "I just kept using what I had"

**Energy transitions** — the moments when energy shifts significantly — are especially informative. When a customer's language suddenly becomes more active and detailed, something happened. Ask about it.

"I notice you started being much more specific around [point]. What changed at that moment?"

### Energy Mapping Template

After the interview, assign an energy level (1-5) to each stage and note what drove the shift:

```
Stage            | Energy | What drove it
-----------------|--------|----------------
First thought    | [1-5]  | _______________
Passive looking  | [1-5]  | _______________
Active looking   | [1-5]  | _______________
Deciding moment  | [1-5]  | _______________
Big hire         | [1-5]  | _______________
First use        | [1-5]  | _______________
Settled state    | [1-5]  | _______________
```

## The "Tell Me More" Discipline

The most powerful probing phrase in JTBD interviewing is not a question — it is an instruction: "Tell me more."

"Tell me more" works because:
- It doesn't suggest a direction. The customer decides what to elaborate on.
- It signals genuine interest without implying evaluation.
- It creates space for the customer to add the detail they edited out the first time.
- It avoids the leading question trap ("So that was frustrating?" already contains an interpretation).

**When to use "tell me more":**
- When a customer gives a vague or summary response
- When you sense emotional content that wasn't fully expressed
- When the customer mentions something in passing that could be significant
- When you want to stay with a moment longer without steering

**Variations:**
- "Say more about that."
- "What do you mean when you say [exact word they used]?"
- "Help me understand what that was like."
- "What was actually happening when that happened?"

**What not to do:**
- Don't say "interesting" before asking the follow-up — it signals evaluation
- Don't immediately rephrase what they said ("So what I hear you saying is...") — it cuts off further elaboration
- Don't ask a leading follow-up that suggests the answer

## Avoiding Premature Synthesis

The biggest mistake interviewers make is synthesizing during the interview — forming conclusions while the customer is still talking and then asking questions that probe those conclusions rather than following the story.

Premature synthesis sounds like:
- "So it sounds like the main issue was X, right?"
- "Would you say that's the biggest factor?"
- "I'm hearing that [interpretation] — is that accurate?"

Each of these closes off the conversation rather than opening it. The customer hears your interpretation and either confirms it to be agreeable or argues with it — neither of which advances the story.

**The discipline:** During the interview, your job is documentation, not analysis. Capture the story. Notice things that seem important. Flag them for follow-up. But do not draw conclusions out loud.

After the interview, take 30 minutes before reviewing your notes with fresh eyes. Synthesis happens after, not during.

**Practical tools for staying out of synthesis during the interview:**
- Keep a "hypotheses forming" column in your notes that you don't act on until after
- When you feel the urge to reflect back an interpretation, ask for another specific event instead
- If you're running out of time and feel the pressure to "close" on an insight, resist it

## Note-Taking in the Moment

Notes taken during the interview serve three purposes: capturing verbatim quotes, tracking the timeline, and flagging things to follow up on.

### What to Capture

1. **Verbatim quotes** — When a customer uses vivid, specific language, capture it exactly. Do not paraphrase. The exact words are data. "I was so done with it" tells you something different than "I was frustrated with it."

2. **Timeline markers** — Note the approximate timing of each stage. Even rough dates help you understand how long the decision took.

3. **Force signals** — When you hear Push, Pull, Anxiety, or Habit, mark them as such. You'll build the forces map after.

4. **Energy signals** — Note when the customer becomes more or less animated. These shifts matter.

5. **Follow-up flags** — Use a symbol (an asterisk or a box) to mark things you want to come back to. Don't interrupt the flow to follow up immediately.

### What Not to Do

- Don't type so loudly or visibly that the customer feels transcribed
- Don't lose eye contact (or presence on video) for long stretches
- Don't paraphrase in your notes — if you're not sure you got it right, ask them to repeat it
- Don't take notes on your hypotheses during the interview; those go in a separate column after

## Synthesizing After Multiple Interviews

A single interview is a hypothesis. Patterns across multiple interviews are findings.

### The Cross-Interview Pattern Protocol

After completing each interview, fill out the synthesis template from [switch-interview.md](../workflows/switch-interview.md). After every 3rd or 4th interview, do a cross-interview synthesis.

**Step 1: Lay out the struggling moments side by side**

What was the first thought across each interview? Look for:
- The same or similar triggering events
- The same or similar emotional language
- The same or different contexts (are these in the same situation or different ones?)

**Step 2: Map forces across interviews**

Build a simple matrix:

```
                 Push    Pull    Anxiety    Habit
Interview 1:    [H/M/L] [H/M/L] [H/M/L]   [H/M/L]
Interview 2:    [H/M/L] [H/M/L] [H/M/L]   [H/M/L]
Interview 3:    [H/M/L] [H/M/L] [H/M/L]   [H/M/L]
```

Dominant patterns in the matrix are your structural findings.

**Step 3: Find the recurring job**

What progress were these customers trying to make? If you write a job statement for each interview independently, do they converge? If not — are you looking at one job or multiple?

**Step 4: Identify outliers**

Which interviews don't fit the pattern? Outliers are not failures — they are candidates for a distinct customer segment with a different job. Don't average them into the pattern; set them aside and investigate separately.

**Step 5: Generate hypotheses for future interviews**

After cross-interview synthesis, you should have 3-5 hypotheses to test in future interviews. These become the focus areas: "Are we seeing Anxiety as a consistent barrier? Let's probe that harder next time."

### When to Stop Interviewing

You're ready to synthesize findings when:
- New interviews are confirming existing patterns without adding new material
- You can predict what the next customer will say about the struggling moment before they say it
- The forces map is stable — new interviews aren't shifting the dominant forces

This is called theoretical saturation. It typically happens around 12-15 interviews in a well-defined segment.
